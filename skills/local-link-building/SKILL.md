# Local Link Building

Distilled from the Advanced Link Training course (Bill Hartzer) for a single
local service business (plumbing, home services, etc.). Self-contained: this
skill has everything needed to plan and run a local link program.

## The one test for any tactic

> **"If you had to describe exactly what you did, in detail, to the search
> engine whose rankings you are trying to influence, would you have anything to
> explain away?"**

Not "would you get caught" and not "does it work." Two modifiers: does the
tactic still function mechanically? Does risk scale with volume or is it fixed?
(Volume-scaling risk = situational; fixed risk = high risk regardless of
execution.)

**Default stance for local: earned over placed.** Local link building is the
highest-probability link building there is — any business with an address or
service area can do it. But it doesn't delegate well: the owner outperforms an
agency. 15–20 genuine local domains already leads most markets.

## The local link building playbook (in order)

### 1. Fix your own foundation first (zero outreach, highest ROI)
- **Canonical NAP** (Name, Address, Phone) — one exact version, everywhere. Fix
  this before anything else; duplicates beat creating new ones.
- **Claim the primary platforms**: Google Business Profile, Apple, Bing, Yelp,
  aggregators, chamber. Nofollowed citations still help local visibility.
- **Fix internal linking** — most local sites gain more here than from 20 more
  external links.
- **Link reclamation** — recover links you had and lost. 97.4% of lost links
  vanished while the source page stayed live (only 4 of 645,202 went to a 404).
  Links are *edited away*, not deleted with their pages. This is the
  best-evidenced, most underused tactic. Start here.

### 2. Inventory who already knows you
- Suppliers' dealer locators, partner pages, vendor pages — you may already be
  listed without a link.
- **Testimonial links**: write a real endorsement for a supplier you use; the
  vendor publishes it with a linked credit. ~20 minutes per link from established
  relevant domains. Never ask for keyword anchors. One name endorsing dozens of
  unrelated products is a permanent footprint — keep it genuine and few.

### 3. Join the obvious local bodies
- **Chamber of commerce** — membership usually includes a linked listing.
- Local associations, trade bodies, council/community sites.
- **Resource page link building**: ask curators of existing link lists (library
  guides, association pages, council sites) to include your resource. Lists hold
  references, tools, datasets — not landing pages. Find pages with operators
  (`intitle:resources "topic"`, `site:.org inurl:resources`), read the list and
  the curator's standards, find the named maintainer. Nofollow institutional
  links are still worth taking; one good inclusion propagates as other curators
  copy the list.

### 4. Sponsor something local and real
- **Community & sponsorship links**: sponsor pages and supporter lists of
  organizations you actually support. One or two a year is normal; forty across
  unrelated regions is a footprint.
- Start from real connections → qualify the organization → offer what they need
  (in-kind beats money) → ask for brand-name anchors only.
- The line: would the link make sense if search didn't exist? Commercial anchors
  and far-away sponsorships turn it into a paid-link pattern. Local coverage
  matters more than the listing.

### 5. Feed local press stories with photos
- **Event link building**: host, sponsor, speak at, or organize local events,
  then collect the listings, bios, coverage, and recaps. Speaking is cheapest per
  link; hosting is most control. Build a permanent event page on your own domain,
  submit to calendars 6–8 weeks out, give partners copy with the URL. Listing
  links decay within a year; the durable assets are speaker bios, coverage, recaps.
- **Local press**: give them a real story with photos. Proximity is a news value —
  "affects this outlet's readers where they live." A local angle (regional
  breakdown, local data) is what makes a story run locally.

### 6. Unlinked mention reclamation
- Find places your brand is named without a link and ask for the link. Best
  conversion rate in outreach (well-chosen asks convert far above a third).
- Ask when a reader would plausibly click (data cited, product named, competitors
  linked); leave it when the publisher never links out or a link would read as
  promotion. Set up brand alerts, sweep historical mentions, verify there's
  really no link on the rendered page, sort into ask / leave / monitor.

### 7. Competitor link gap analysis
- Compare your profile against 3–5 *search* competitors (not commercial rivals)
  to find domains linking to them but not you. Domain-level intersect, overlap
  threshold of two. Only finds opportunities someone else already created — pair
  with the tactics above. Never copy a competitor's paid links.

## Prospecting (before any email)

- **Target set first** — write, in prose, who would cite you and why, before
  opening any tool. Three questions: (1) Who already writes about this subject
  for an audience that is not me? (2) What do I have that gives them a reason to
  point at me? (3) What specific page (URL, not domain) would they link to?
  A real set is usually 150–400 domains; 5,000 is too loose.
- **Search operators** narrow the crawl: one topic term + one page-type signal +
  exclusions. `"plumbing" inurl:resources -inurl:blog -inurl:category -inurl:tag`.
  Working: `site:`, `inurl:`, `intitle:`, quotes, `-`, uppercase `OR`, `*`,
  `filetype:`. Dead: `inanchor:`, `related:`, `+`, `~`.
- **Find the actual human**, not the contact form. Verify the address (published
  somewhere beats a guessed pattern; catch-all domains prove nothing). Never
  guess email patterns — bounces damage sending reputation.
- **Qualify in two passes**: Pass 1 — binary disqualifiers as a *delete* (already
  links to you, no outbound editorial links, sells links, unrelated vertical, not
  indexed, dead, language/region mismatch, direct competitor, no contact route).
  Pass 2 — score survivors on relevance to the *destination page* (weight
  highest), evidence they link to sites like yours, plausible mechanism, authority
  as tiebreaker only. Sort into Tier A (bespoke pitch), Tier B (structured
  template), Tier C (never emailed).
- The most common cause of failed outreach = a bad list, not bad writing.

## Outreach (the pitch)

Outreach is a **relevance problem, not a persuasion problem.** Numbers don't fix
a bad list. Response rate is a property of fit, and it falls as volume rises.

**The 5-sentence pitch** (under 150 words, plain text, no tracking pixel):
1. The specific observation with the URL, straight in — no greeting-compliment.
2. Why it matters to *them* (their page, their readers).
3. What you have, one line, nothing about your company.
4. The explicit, singular ask.
5. The exit: "No reply needed if it is not a fit."

Sign off with a real name and a two-line signature. No opening compliment, no
company intro, no credentials list, no attachment. Subject lines describe a
problem on the recipient's own site in 6–7 sentence-case words; never "Quick
question," fake Re:, or the words partnership/collaboration/opportunity.

**Follow-up policy**: one follow-up at 4–7 days; a second only with genuinely new
information, at 10–14 days; never a third. Three touches is persistence, five is
harassment. The follow-up must add something, reduce the ask, or close cleanly
("I will not follow up again"). Never "just bumping this up your inbox."

**Deliverability is infrastructure**: SPF + DKIM + DMARC, send cold outreach from
a separate honest variant domain (with a real single page, aged a few weeks),
warm up over weeks, cap at 20–50 per address per day. An email that doesn't
arrive was rejected before the recipient saw it. Diagnose delivery before
rewriting the pitch.

**Track**: one row per domain, fixed outcome vocabulary (linked, agreed not
live, refused with reason, refused, wrong contact, contact later, do not contact,
no response, bounced), plus a next-action date. Track response rate AND placement
rate (placement = live links; always lower). Refusals with reasons are the most
under-collected data in link building.

## The 90-day plan (local)

- **Weeks 1–2**: research only. Dated referring-domain snapshot, gap analysis on
  3–5 competitors, qualify every row, find the person, decide the offer. Zero email.
- **Weeks 3–4**: build the asset for the list you actually have (a resource page,
  a local data page, a guide). Finish it — a half-built asset is worse than a
  smaller complete one.
- **Weeks 5–12**: 15–30 first contacts/week, written for the recipient. Week 7
  first follow-ups land on top of new contacts (workload doubles — don't stop).
  Record every outcome including refusals. Week 11 stop new contacts.
- **Target: 5 to 15 genuine referring domains.** That is a good result, not a
  disappointing one. Judge at week 16, not day 90 — placements land in weeks 10–16.
- **Replacement math**: the profile sheds ~1/6 of domains per year. 10 domains
  gained roughly replaces a fifth of a year's decay. Growth needs a higher rate
  or better links — and better links are the cheaper lever (high-trust domains
  last ~4× longer).

**Failure modes** (diagnose before changing tactic): wrong prospects (reply rate
near zero → re-qualify a sample); nothing worth linking to (replies healthy,
links/replies near zero → rebuild the asset); templated outreach (curt/hostile
replies → write ten by hand); no follow-up (schedule both dates when the first
email is sent); wrong metric (reporting total backlinks hides real work);
stopping at three weeks (the worst-looking moment of a good campaign).

## What to avoid (local)

- **Paid links / niche edits / link insertions** — high risk, never for ranking
  credit. A link's value tracks the host page's own inbound links, not its age.
- **Link exchanges** ("you link to me, I'll link to you") — named almost verbatim
  in Google's link spam policies; the cheapest pattern to detect. Never.
- **Bulk directory submission** — general web directories are dead since DMOZ
  closed. Citations are fine; "submit to 500 directories" packages are the oldest
  junk still being sold.
- **Buying a prospect list** — encodes someone else's relevance, sold to your
  niche, inherits recipients' fatigue.
- **.edu / scholarship links** — a genuine 20-year profile of 22,260 referring
  domains contains five .edu links and zero .gov. .edu carries no ranking bonus.
- **Anything sold as "a list of sites that will link to you"** — a published list
  dies the week it's published.

## Measurement

- **Count referring domains, not links.** One sitewide placement can be 16.8% of
  a profile from a single decision. The top 100 domains = 84.3% of links.
- **Referral traffic is the only effect you measure directly and completely.**
  Report what was done, what it produced, what it cost, what's next — in numbers
  you can defend.
- **Unnatural profile tells**: exact-match anchor patterns on commercial pages,
  low trust ratio, dense interlinked clusters, footprints (shared hosting/theme/
  registrant), link velocity spikes with no news event to explain them.
- **Disavow** is for manual actions / genuinely manipulative history — NOT for
  third-party "toxicity" scores. Disavowing on a vendor score routinely removes
  value from good links.
- **Links decay**: median referring domain stops linking after ~1,080 days; 49.6%
  of all links ever recorded are lost. Reclamation is always available.

## Local-specific notes

- **The owner outperforms an agency** — local link building doesn't delegate well.
  The owner knows the chamber, the suppliers, the local press, the community.
- **The well runs dry annually** — local sources get exhausted; pair with tactics
  that scale (reclamation, unlinked mentions, gap analysis) so you're not
  re-pitching the same chamber every year.
- **Nofollow is not "as good as" followed** — it's that all else is rarely equal.
  A nofollow local news mention with real reach beats a followed link from a
  dead blog. Don't refuse, pay for, or fight nofollow coverage.
- **Digital PR is usually the wrong choice for a small local service site** —
  budget is better spent on sponsorships and a proper site. Local press works
  through proximity and photos, not national data studies.
- **Citations are infrastructure** — consistency rate and duplicate count are the
  metrics, not the number of listings.
