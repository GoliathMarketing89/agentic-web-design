# Patterns

## Be Simple, Be Direct, Don't Be Fancy
**When to use**: You are first in your market (Stage 1 sophistication) or the market is unsophisticated.
**How**: State the need or claim in the headline, nothing more. Dramatize the claim in copy, bring in the product, prove it works. "NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!"
**Trade-offs**: Simple and powerful, but only works while your story is brand-new; fails once competitors enter.

## Enlarge the Claim to the Limit
**When to use**: You're second and the direct claim is still working (Stage 2).
**How**: Copy the successful claim but drive it to the absolute limit of legality and believability. "LOSE UP TO 47 POUNDS IN 4 WEEKS — OR RECEIVE $40 BACK!"
**Trade-offs**: Works until the believability limit is reached; then claims get discounted 50% and the market moves to Stage 3.

## Introduce a New Mechanism
**When to use**: The market has heard all the claims in all their extremes (Stage 3).
**How**: Shift emphasis from what the product does to HOW it works. Move the mechanism into the headline; restate the claim in full in the lead paragraph. "FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!"
**Trade-offs**: Gives only a temporary advantage; competitors adopt the mechanism and it moves to Stage 4.

## Elaborate the Mechanism
**When to use**: A competitor has just introduced a new mechanism that's producing sales (Stage 4).
**How**: Elaborate/enlarge the successful mechanism — make it easier, quicker, surer; solve more of the problem; overcome old limitations; promise extra benefits. "FIRST NO-DIET REDUCING WONDER DRUG!"
**Trade-offs**: Same limitation as Stage 2 — eventually pushes out of believability.

## Shift to Identification
**When to use**: The field is exhausted; no new mechanism gains acceptance (Stage 5).
**How**: Bring the prospect into the ad through identification, not desire. Write an identification headline that echoes an emotion, attitude, or satisfaction.
**Trade-offs**: The most difficult and least guided; easy to write an empty headline. The prospect must identify with it before he can buy from it.

## Build a Believability-Bridge / Gradualization
**When to use**: A claim is too powerful or too new to be believed raw.
**How**: Start with facts the prospect already accepts; lead him through a gradual succession of more remote facts. Use inclusion questions, detailed identification, contradiction of false beliefs, the language of logic, syllogistic thinking, paragraph parallelism.
**Trade-offs**: Requires research to know the reader's beliefs; if you violate established beliefs, nothing you promise can save the ad.

## Redefine the Objection Away
**When to use**: The product is too complicated, not important enough, or costs too much.
**How**: Simplification (make the hard seem easy), Escalation (broaden importance/benefits), Price Reduction (switch the comparison to a more expensive standard).
**Trade-offs**: Subtle and powerful, but operates below the conscious mind; must be accurate or it shatters believability.

## Prove the Mechanism
**When to use**: The prospect demands "How does it work?"
**How**: Name the mechanism (when understood), describe it (when not), or feature it in the headline (when it's your point of difference). Never let mechanism copy become dull — load it with promise and emotion.
**Trade-offs**: The question is never "should I use it" but "how much" — depends on the state of awareness.

## Concentrate on the Competition
**When to use**: Your budget is smaller than a competitor's, or most prospects are already his customers.
**How**: Point out competitors' weaknesses, emphasize their disservice, then prove your product gives him what he wants without them. Never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution at the same time. Use bad-good-bad-good interweaving contrast.
**Trade-offs**: Takes sizable space; if you attack without providing the solution, you evoke skepticism and dislike.

## Camouflage to Borrow Believability
**When to use**: To overcome the automatic skepticism that "advertising language" triggers.
**How**: Adopt the host publication's format, adopt its phraseology/style, or use the moods of Understatement and Deadly Sincerity.
**Trade-offs**: A single format change can add 50% to readership; but the ad must be adapted per medium, which costs extra.

## The Catalog (Summarize and Hedge)
**When to use**: After intensifying a single dominant desire, to hedge and close the sale.
**How**: List all the product's performances/benefits/applications in condensed single lines. Two types: expand desire horizontally (applications) or vertically (deepen desire).
**Trade-offs**: Shifts from rifle (single dominant appeal) to shotgun (many appeals); useful as a last-minute hedge.

## Put Your Guarantee to Work
**When to use**: As you close the sale and ask for action.
**How**: Turn the guarantee into the climax of the ad — a last summary of the product's performances, reinforced at every step by the positive reassertion of the guarantee.
**Trade-offs**: Powerful closer; must be specific and credible.

## The Imitation Game
**When to use**: when starting out or lacking inspiration.
**How**: write a brief → research trending headlines → formulate by imitating their style → develop by analyzing why they work → adapt to your topic → expand with a benefit modifier.
**Trade-offs**: fast start and proven structure, but risks copying if you don't adapt.

## Amazon R&D
**When to use**: to find what's enticing people in your niche.
**How**: write a brief → browse Amazon best sellers in your niche → map the subtitle structure → expand it for your niche.
**Trade-offs**: great for promises and positioning, but subtitles are book-specific.

## Community Arbitrage
**When to use**: to get into the reader's head and find real pains/desires.
**How**: find a related book → read "most helpful" reviews → highlight inner-dialog and emotional points → extract into headlines and bullets.
**Trade-offs**: authentic voice, but requires manual review mining.

## Cognitive Manipulation
**When to use**: to overcome bias-related objections.
**How**: apply anchoring, bandwagon, choice-supportive, pro-innovation, and zero-risk biases.
**Trade-offs**: powerful, but must stay ethical — persuasion over manipulation.

## The Kintsugi Revision Process
**When to use**: on the second draft.
**How**: shorten sentences, remove redundancy, add metaphor and emotion.
**Trade-offs**: improves quality, but requires a separate editing pass.

## The Vocal Editing Technique
**When to use**: after the Kintsugi pass.
**How**: read the copy aloud; revise where you stumble.
**Trade-offs**: catches awkward phrasing, but needs a quiet space.

## Feature-Benefit Exercise
**When to use**: whenever you're tempted to list features.
**How**: every feature must be answered with "You benefit by..." — sell the benefit, not the attribute.
**Trade-offs**: forces WIIFM clarity; can feel repetitive if overdone.

## Directing Mental Movies
**When to use**: to build desire and demonstrate the product in the mind.
**How**: heighten the five internal representations (V-A-K-O-G) with vivid sensory language; the more brain space you occupy, the more you persuade.
**Trade-offs**: why long copy outsells short; requires specific, visual writing.

## The Granny Rule of Direct Mail
**When to use**: direct mail and email.
**How**: make it personal, handwritten, conversational; personalize salutations ("Dear Bob" not "Dear Friend"); start with a question and a positive benefit; use AIDA.
**Trade-offs**: boosts open/read rates; not suited to mass impersonal formats.

## Extreme Specificity
**When to use**: to differentiate and build desire.
**How**: describe your product with extreme, concrete detail; educate prospects about what you do and how you do it. The one who says it wins, even if everyone does it.
**Trade-offs**: requires real detail; can't fake specificity.

## Scarcity Deadline
**When to use**: every offer that can carry a deadline.
**How**: hard deadlines ("Sale Ends August 21st") or soft ("Quantities Strictly Limited"); without one, your offer reads as always available.
**Trade-offs**: breaks inertia; overuse erodes trust.
