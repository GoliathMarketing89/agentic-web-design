# Chapter 9: Headlines — Mechanics, Simplicity, and Pulling the Prospect In

## Core Idea
Your headline has only one job — to stop your prospect and compel him to read the second sentence of your ad. It does not need to sell, mention your product, or even state your main appeal. The headline is the bridge between your prospect and your product, and it must start at the point of awareness the prospect has reached. When you're first in a market, be simple, be direct, don't be fancy. The headline's power comes from the strategy behind it (the three questions), not from cleverness.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Headline's Real Job**: to stop the prospect and force him to read the second sentence; the second sentence forces the third, and so on. Use it to decide what a headline must do — not sell, but pull the reader in.
  - When to use: whenever you're tempted to put the full selling burden on the headline.
  - How: make the headline the first step in a chain that leads the reader through the whole ad.
- **The Headline as Bridge**: the headline starts at the prospect's point of awareness. If he's aware of your product, start with the product; if he's aware only of the desire, start with the desire; if he's aware only of a general problem, start with the problem and crystallize it into a specific need. Use it to decide where the headline begins.
  - When to use: after answering the three questions, to locate the point of entry.
- **Be Simple, Be Direct, Don't Be Fancy**: the strategy for a first-stage (unsophisticated) market. State the need or claim in the headline, nothing more. Use it when you're first in your field.
  - How: "NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!" — the simplest statement of the direct desire.
- **The Five States of Awareness and Their Headlines**: the awareness scale determines what the headline must do.
  - Most Aware: name the product and a bargain price ("Revere Zomar Lens, Electric Eye Camera — Formerly $149.50 — Now Only $119.95").
  - Customer Knows of Product: display the name and point out superiority (seven tasks: reinforce desire, sharpen image, extend where/when, introduce new proof, announce new mechanism, eliminate limitations, change the image).
  - New Products: name the desire and/or its solution, prove it can be accomplished, show the mechanism is in your product.
  - Products That Solve Needs: name the need and/or its solution, dramatize the need, present the product as the inevitable solution.
  - Completely Unaware: an identification headline that calls the market together — echo an emotion, attitude, or satisfaction that picks people out and binds them together.
- **Verbalization (The 38 Ways to Strengthen Your Headline)**: the art of increasing the impact of a headline by the way it is stated. Use it when you're competitive or the thought is too complicated to state simply.
  - How: bind other images to the main claim with the words you use. The 38 ways include: measure the size of the claim, measure the speed, compare, metaphorize, sensitize, demonstrate, dramatize, state as paradox, remove limitations, associate with values/people, show how much work it does, state as a question, offer information, tie in authority, before-and-after, stress newness, stress exclusivity, turn into a challenge, case-history quotation, condense, symbolize, connect mechanism to claim, startle by contradicting, connect need and claim, offer information, turn into case history, name the problem, warn about pitfalls, emphasize by phraseology, show how easy, state the difference, surprise into realizing limitations overcome, address people who can't buy, address the prospect directly, dramatize how hard it was to produce, accuse the claim of being too good, challenge limiting beliefs, turn into question-and-answer.
- **The Three Levels of Creativity**: the approaches copy writers use to dig up a new headline.
  - Word-Substitute Technique (shallowest): pull a proven headline and substitute your own product. Produces "Echo Ads" that remind people of other products.
  - Formulas: pour your headline into memorized rules. Valid for verbalization, but the idea demands its own shape.
  - Analysis (the only real way): the three-question approach with guideposts, not molds.

## Key Concepts
- **Headline**: the first step in pulling the prospect into the ad; its only job is to get the second sentence read.
- **State of Awareness**: how much the prospect knows about your product and the satisfaction it performs; determines where the headline starts.
- **Identification Headline**: a headline that calls the market together by echoing an emotion, attitude, or satisfaction — used for unaware markets.
- **Verbalization**: increasing a headline's impact by how it's stated.
- **Echo Ads**: ads that remind people of some other product because they substitute words into a proven headline without respecting the unique product-market-timing relationship.
- **The Creative Gamble**: risking the headline (not mentioning the product) to grasp attention and impel the reader into the body copy.
- **The Cardinal Rule of the Fifth Stage**: your prospect must identify with your headline before he can buy from it — it must be his headline, his problem, his state of mind.

## Mental Models
- Use "the headline's only job is to get the second sentence read" to resist overloading it.
- Think of the headline as a bridge that starts on the market's side (awareness) and leads to the product's side.
- Use "be simple, be direct, don't be fancy" when you're first in a market.
- Think of the five awareness states as a scale — the more aware the market, the less you need to say.
- Use "your prospect must identify with your headline before he can buy from it" for unaware markets.

## Anti-patterns
- **Putting the full selling burden on the headline**: it's only 10-20% of the ad's space; the job is to pull the reader into the body copy.
- **Being fancy when you're first**: a simple direct claim beats cleverness in an unsophisticated market.
- **Using a headline from the wrong awareness stage**: a headline that works at one stage fails at another, and fails even in its own market once it moves on.
- **Word-substitution**: produces Echo Ads that lose the strength of the unique product-market-timing relationship.
- **Writing an empty fifth-stage headline**: a startler, attention-getter, or cute headline with no real identification wastes the client's money.
- **Mentioning price, product, function, or desire to an unaware market**: none of these mean anything to someone who doesn't know or want your product.

## Worked Example
The Television Repair Manual ad shows the headline as bridge. The power-claim headline "Save up to $100 a Year on Your TV Repairs!" failed because prospects saw themselves as non-handymen. The revised headline — "WHY HAVEN'T TV OWNERS BEEN TOLD THESE FACTS?" — made no claim and no promise except disclosure, but it crystallized the universal resentment against high repair bills and service contracts. It was a headline the prospect could agree with, and it pulled him into the body copy where the ad built acceptance step by step. The "THEY LAUGHED WHEN I SAT DOWN AT THE PIANO" ad shows the identification headline for an unaware market — it projects an ultimate triumph the prospect identifies with, selling nothing but the information that follows.

## Key Takeaways
1. The headline's only job is to stop the prospect and get the second sentence read.
2. Start the headline at the prospect's point of awareness — product, desire, or problem.
3. Be simple, be direct, don't be fancy when you're first in a market.
4. Use verbalization (the 38 ways) to strengthen a headline when you're competitive.
5. For unaware markets, write an identification headline the prospect can identify with before he can buy from it.
6. Avoid word-substitution — it produces Echo Ads that ignore the unique product-market-timing relationship.
7. The headline is the end of one road and the beginning of another; it's the invitation to your ad.
