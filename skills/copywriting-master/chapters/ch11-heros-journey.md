# Chapter 11: The Hero's Journey & The Diamond of Persuasion

## Core Idea
Your audience is the hero of their own story; you are the guide (mentor). Map where each reader sits on the Hero's Journey and adjust your copy to that phase, and use the Diamond of Persuasion's five strategies to make them cry and make them buy.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Hero's Journey (12 phases)**: adapted from Joseph Campbell's "The Hero With A Thousand Faces" (via Jonah Sachs' "Story Wars" and Christopher Vogler's "The Writer's Journey").
  - When to use: to understand your audience's path and to generate copywriting prompts for any campaign.
  - How: different parts of your audience are on different phases; adjust your writing to where they are.
- **The Diamond of Persuasion (5 strategies)**: Fan Their Flames, Pardon Their Errors, Defeat Their Demons, Prove Their Presumptions, Attack Their Enemies.
  - When to use: as a template/outline for any persuasive piece; start on any side and move down the line, or make one the primary focus.
  - How: combined they form the strongest element of persuasion in human nature.

## Key Concepts
- **The Ordinary World**: the hero's starting point — small, unqualified, unaware of their potential.
- **Call to Adventure**: the opportunity to change.
- **Refusal of the Call**: resistance to change; the hero often chooses avoidance over transcendence.
- **Meeting the Mentor**: where you (the brand) enter as guide.
- **Crossing the Threshold / Tests, Allies, Enemies / Approach the Dragon's Den / The Ordeal / The Return**: the middle and resolution phases.
- **Higher-level values**: the common factor in all heroes — the deep, often unrealized pursuit of values (relationships, peace of mind, legacy/influence, time, attention).
- **Fan Their Flames**: help the audience understand the gap between where they are and where they want to be; inspire rebellion against self-doubt, not entitlement.
- **Pardon Their Errors**: forgive the reader's past mistakes so they can move forward.
- **Defeat Their Demons**: help them conquer their internal fears and obstacles.
- **Prove Their Presumptions**: validate what they already believe to be true.
- **Attack Their Enemies**: give them a shared enemy to fight alongside you.

## Mental Models
- Use the Hero's Journey when you need to understand your audience's world; use the Diamond of Persuasion when you need to structure the emotional appeal.
- Think of your brand as the mentor, not the hero — "You are not the hero, your audience is."
- Think of the gap: the product/service fills the gap between where the hero is and where they want to be.
- Use "change is scary" — avoid asking for too much change early; present a new journey rather than forcing change.

## Anti-patterns
- **Making the brand the hero**: blows up with hype then evaporates; the audience must wear the cape.
- **Shallow inspiration**: "you deserve it" / "just do it" with no guidance on how to get it — encourages entitlement, not rebellion.
- **Forcing change**: change requires more effort than doing something new; present a new path instead.
- **Unethical manipulation**: manipulation relies on false promises and distorted facts; persuasion relies on skillful demonstration of the facts.

## Worked Example
The Apple example: the lesson that "the audience is the hero" is what created the first trillion-dollar brand. Everyone who harnesses this power can build an empire, and those who ignore it are stuck wondering what they're doing wrong. The copy's job is to reflect back to the hero a detailed image of their inner self — a "mirror" clear enough to shed new light — so they trust you as their new guide.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define your hero's backstory: their fears, known and unknown, and their higher-level values.
2. Reflect the hero's image back to them accurately — respond to truth, not a romanticized version.
3. Acknowledge the sacrifice and change the hero must make to realize their values.
4. Use the Diamond's five strategies as a checklist for emotional persuasion.
5. Don't literally write a story every time — weave the hero's attributes into any piece.
