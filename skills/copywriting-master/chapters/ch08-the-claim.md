# Chapter 8: The Claim

## Core Idea
The claim is the promise of performance you make to your prospect — the statement of what your product does to satisfy his dominant desire. It is the heart of your ad: everything else (headline, mechanism, proof, identification) exists to make the claim believed. The claim is not static — it must be built, strengthened, and refreshed as your market moves through the stages of sophistication, or it loses its power.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Claim as the Dominant Performance**: the claim is the one performance of your product that harnesses the greatest sales power at that time. Every product has dozens of performances; only one can be featured. Use it to decide what your ad promises.
  - When to use: after selecting the mass desire, to choose the single performance that satisfies it most deeply.
  - How: list the product's performances, group them against the desires they satisfy, feature the one that taps the greatest desire.
- **Building the Claim (Stage 1)**: when you're first, state the claim simply and directly — nothing more. "NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!" Use it when the market is unsophisticated and your story is brand-new.
  - How: state the need or claim in the headline, dramatize it in copy, bring in the product, prove it works.
- **Strengthening the Claim (Stage 2)**: enlarge the claim, drive it to the absolute limit, outbid your competition. "LOSE UP TO 47 POUNDS IN 4 WEEKS — OR RECEIVE $40 BACK!" Use it when the direct claim is still working but competitors are entering.
  - How: push the claim to the outer limits of legality and believability.
- **Refreshing the Claim (Stage 3)**: when the claim is overworked and no longer believed, give it a NEW MECHANISM — a new way to make the old promise work. "FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!" Use it when the market has heard all the claims in all their extremes.
  - How: shift emphasis from what the product does to HOW it works; move the mechanism into the headline; restate the claim in full in the lead paragraph.
- **The Point of Difference**: the new mechanism, quality, or freedom from limitation that gives the claim a fresh basis for believability. Use it to invade an established field.
  - How: introduce a new mechanism that performs the claim, a new quality that assures its performance, or a new freedom from old limitations.
- **The Physical Product as Claim Support**: physical facts (weight of steel, number of tubes, stitches per inch) never become the headline, but they document and reinforce the claim. Use them to justify price, document quality, assure continuity, sharpen the mental picture, and give the claim a fresh basis for believability.

## Key Concepts
- **Claim**: the promise of performance — what your product does to satisfy the dominant desire.
- **Dominant Performance**: the one performance featured in the ad; the claim.
- **Mechanism**: the way the claim is accomplished; the new device that makes an old claim fresh.
- **Point of Difference**: the new mechanism/quality/freedom that distinguishes your product and refreshes the claim.
- **Believability**: the acceptance the claim receives; the ultimate goal of all copy is conviction.
- **Verbalization**: the art of increasing the impact of a claim by the way it is stated (the 38 ways).
- **The Claim's Life Cycle**: build (Stage 1) → strengthen (Stage 2) → refresh with mechanism (Stage 3) → elaborate mechanism (Stage 4) → shift to identification (Stage 5).

## Mental Models
- Use "the claim is the promise; everything else is proof" to keep your ad focused.
- Think of the claim as needing constant refreshment — a claim that worked at one stage of sophistication won't work at the next.
- Use "the physical product documents, the functional product sells" when deciding what supports the claim.
- Think of the mechanism as the claim's second wind — it makes an old promise new and believable again.

## Anti-patterns
- **Letting the claim go stale**: a claim that worked when you were first will fail once the market has heard it a hundred times.
- **Leading with the physical product**: physical facts document the claim but never become the headline.
- **Piling on promises instead of strengthening belief**: one fully-believed promise outsells ten partially-believed ones.
- **Using a claim the prospect can't believe raw**: without supporting evidence already in his mind, a powerful claim is rejected as exaggerated or false.
- **Forgetting the claim when you shift to mechanism**: even at Stage 3, the claim must be restated in full so the prospect realizes everything he's getting.

## Worked Example
The reducing field shows the claim's full life cycle. The original claim was "NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!" — a simple direct statement for a first-stage market. As competitors entered, the claim was strengthened: "LOSE UP TO 47 POUNDS IN 4 WEEKS — OR RECEIVE $40 BACK!" and "I AM 61 POUNDS LIGHTER... NEVER A HUNGRY MINUTE." When the market had heard all the claims, the claim was refreshed with a mechanism: "FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!" — where the mechanism moved into the headline and the claim was restated in the lead paragraph ("melts up to 37 POUNDS off men and women — without starvation diets, without a single hungry moment"). The spark plug ad shows the physical product supporting the claim: the copy describes the filthy, corroded firing point of an old plug robbing the car of 20 horsepower, then presents the new plug that "ELIMINATES THAT DEADLY CARBON" — the mechanism giving the claim a fresh basis for believability.

## Key Takeaways
1. The claim is the promise of performance — the one dominant performance that satisfies the dominant desire.
2. Build it simply when you're first; strengthen it to the limit when you're second.
3. Refresh it with a new mechanism when the market has heard all the claims.
4. Use the physical product to document and reinforce the claim, never to lead with.
5. One fully-believed claim outsells ten partially-believed ones — invest in believability, not volume.
6. The claim has a life cycle tied to market sophistication; refresh it or lose it.
