# Chapter 1: What People Really Want

## Core Idea
People care first about themselves, not about you or your company. The strongest advertising appeals tap the eight biologically hardwired "Life-Force" desires, because you can't shake a desire you were born with.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Life-Force 8 (LF8)**: The eight primary human desires hardwired into every brain, responsible for more sales than all other wants combined:
  1. Survival, enjoyment of life, life extension.
  2. Enjoyment of food and beverages.
  3. Freedom from fear, pain, and danger.
  4. Sexual companionship.
  5. Comfortable living conditions.
  6. To be superior, winning, keeping up with the Joneses.
  7. Care and protection of loved ones.
  8. Social approval.
  - When to use: whenever you need a benefit claim or appeal that will actually move people.
  - How: build your headline and benefit claims around whichever LF8 your product genuinely satisfies.
- **The Nine Learned (Secondary) Human Wants**: Wants we learn, not ones we're born with: (1) to be informed, (2) curiosity, (3) cleanliness of body and surroundings, (4) efficiency, (5) convenience, (6) dependability/quality, (7) expression of beauty and style, (8) economy/profit, (9) bargains.
  - When to use: as supporting appeals only; they are far weaker than the LF8 because we are not biologically driven to satisfy them.
- **Tension → Desire → Action**: The formula for desire. A need unmet creates tension; tension creates desire; desire drives action to satisfy it.
  - When to use: to diagnose why an appeal will or won't motivate.
  - How: create the tension (unmet need), then present your product as the satisfying action.
- **Vicarious Desire Fulfillment**: People enjoy reading about how others satisfied their LF8 desires; it lets them pre-experience the payoff.
  - When to use: in testimonials, case studies, and benefit copy.
  - How: write specific, visual language that lets the reader imagine themselves enjoying the result.

## Key Concepts
- **Life-Force 8 (LF8)**: The eight biologically programmed primary desires that drive more buying than all other wants combined.
- **Secondary (learned) wants**: Nine wants we acquire through learning, not biology; useful but far less bankable than the LF8.
- **Desire**: A type of tension felt when a need isn't met.
- **Vicarious fulfillment**: The pleasure of reading how others satisfied a desire, which lets prospects pre-experience the product.
- **Mental movie**: The internal visual experience your words install in a prospect's head; the first use of any product happens in the consumer's mind.
- **WIIFM**: "What's In It For Me" — the question every prospect is silently asking.
- **Self-demonstration**: Getting the prospect to imagine using the product, which boosts desire and motivation to buy.

## Mental Models
- Use the LF8 when you need an appeal that can't be argued with — you can't shake a desire you were born with.
- Think of desire as tension: create the tension of an unmet need, then present your product as the release.
- Think of your words as a movie projector: the more specific and visual the language, the more brain space you occupy and the more you persuade.
- Use secondary wants when the LF8 don't fit, but never expect them to carry a campaign alone.

## Anti-patterns
- **Advertising your factory, staff, equipment, or "unusual camera angles"**: people don't care about you; they care about what you do for them.
- **Appealing to secondary wants when an LF8 fits**: biology is king; a learned want can't match a hardwired one.
- **Vague, non-visual language**: "Go somewhere and do something" installs no mental movie and persuades no one.
- **Chasing eyeballs over the right eyeballs**: a headline like "SEX!" opens envelopes but attracts the wrong prospects.

## Worked Example
Haldeman-Julius sold 200 million five-cent books in the 1920s-30s by advertising only the titles. When a title underperformed, he changed the title — not the copy — and studied response. The two strongest appeals were sex and self-improvement, both LF8. The lesson: the appeal itself, not the writing, drives response.

Whitman's "Go somewhere and do something" escalation shows how word choice builds imagery: "Go somewhere and do something" (blank screen) → "Go to the kitchen, open the oven, and pull out the freshest, crispiest, most delicious hot pizza you've ever eaten..." Each step adds specific visual words that force the reader to picture the scene, building desire for the product.

## Key Takeaways
1. Base every appeal on one of the Life-Force 8; they are the most powerful motivators because they're biologically programmed.
2. Write copy that makes prospects visually demonstrate your product in their heads — the first use of any product is in the mind.
3. Use specific, visual words (mental movies) to occupy more of the prospect's brain and build desire.
4. Use secondary wants only as supporting appeals, never as the primary driver.
5. After building desire, your next job is credibility (social proof, guarantees), then pushing for action.
