# Chapter 6: The Seven Basic Techniques of Breakthrough Advertising

## Core Idea
Once your headline stops the prospect, body copy does the selling by altering his vision of reality — expanding or altering the three dimensions of his mental world (Desires, Identifications, Beliefs). The seven techniques are the active processes you use to build desire, build a saleable personality, and build belief, layer by layer, until the prospect reaches absolute conviction.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Dimensions of the Prospect's Mind**: Desires (wants, needs, cravings), Identifications (roles the prospect wants to play), and Beliefs (opinions, attitudes, prejudices). Use them to decide what each part of your copy must do.
  - When to use: to plan the structure of body copy — desire copy, identification copy, belief copy.
- **Intensification** (Technique 1): presenting a series of fresh, new, different fulfillments for the prospect's dominant desire. The core of advertising — translating unformulated desire into vivid scenes of fulfillment.
  - When to use: throughout body copy, whenever you need to build desire to overcome skepticism, lethargy, and price.
  - How: 13 ways — present claims directly; put claims in action; bring in the reader; show him how to test your claims; stretch benefits in time; bring in an audience; show experts approving; compare/contrast/prove superiority; picture the black side; show how easy it is; use metaphor/analogy/imagination; summarize (the catalog); put your guarantee to work.
- **Identification** (Technique 2): building a saleable personality into your product by capitalizing on the prospect's longing for expression and recognition, not satisfaction.
  - When to use: when your product does the same job as competitors and price is no longer a factor — the choice then depends on the role your product offers.
  - How: discover the character and achievement roles the prospect will accept, then present them vividly. Build on the product's primary image as a bridge; never contradict accepted images.
- **Gradualization** (Technique 3): making the prospect believe your claims before you state them, by starting with facts he already accepts and leading him through a gradual succession of more remote facts.
  - When to use: whenever a claim is too powerful or too new to be believed raw.
  - How: build a chain of acceptances — inclusion questions, detailed identification, contradiction of false beliefs, the language of logic, syllogistic thinking, contingency structures, repetition of proof, promise-belief-promise variation, paragraph parallelism.
- **Redefinition** (Technique 4): removing objections to your product by giving it a new definition — "this rather than that."
  - When to use: for products that are too complicated, not important enough, or cost too much.
  - How: three types — Simplification (make the hard seem easy), Escalation (broaden the importance/benefits), Price Reduction (switch the comparison to a more expensive standard).
- **Mechanization** (Technique 5): verbally proving that your product does what you claim — the "How does it work?" answer.
  - When to use: whenever the prospect demands proof of mechanism; the question is never "should I use it" but "how much."
  - How: three stages — Name the mechanism (when it's already understood), Describe the mechanism (when it's not), Feature the mechanism (when it's your point of difference and belongs in the headline).
- **Concentration** (Technique 6): destroying alternate ways for the prospect to satisfy his desire by pointing out competitors' weaknesses and proving your product gives him what he wants without them.
  - When to use: when your budget is smaller than a competitor's or most prospects are already his customers — you must crack his image before you can rechannel desire.
  - How: never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution at the same time. Use bad-good-bad-good interweaving contrast, or a "what happens now vs. what happens when you switch" time sequence.
- **Camouflage** (Technique 7): borrowing conviction for your copy from the believability stored in media, formats, phraseology, and moods.
  - When to use: to overcome the automatic skepticism that "advertising language" triggers.
  - How: adopt the host publication's format, adopt its phraseology/style, or use the moods of Understatement (simplicity, few adjectives) and Deadly Sincerity (pointing out flaws so benefits are believed more deeply).

## Key Concepts
- **Desires**: wants, needs, cravings that drive the prospect; you can expand, sharpen, channel, and give them a goal.
- **Identifications**: the roles the prospect wants to play and the personality traits he wants the product to help him build.
- **Beliefs**: the opinions, attitudes, prejudices, and conceptions of reality the prospect lives by; a filter your copy must pass through.
- **Character Roles**: personality traits (virile, chic, brilliant) the prospect wants to express; expressed by adjectives.
- **Achievement Roles**: status/class/position roles (Executive, Wife, Man-On-His-Way-Up) the prospect wants to win and display; expressed by nouns as titles.
- **Primary Image**: the image a product already has in the prospect's mind; the raw material and bridge for building new images.
- **The Catalog**: a brief condensed listing of all the product's performances/benefits/applications, used to summarize and hedge.
- **The Turn**: the delicate transition in copy where the prospect's world is joined to the product.
- **Believability-Bridge**: an accepted image used to connect what the prospect already believes with what you want him to believe.
- **The Language of Logic**: words like "therefore," "here's why," "this has been proved" that carry conviction regardless of content.

## Mental Models
- Use "one fully-believed promise has ten times the sales power of ten partially-believed promises" when deciding whether to add promises or strengthen belief.
- Think of Gradualization as building a bridge of belief from the facts the prospect accepts today to the facts he must accept to buy.
- Use "never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution" as the rule for Concentration.
- Think of Camouflage as tapping stored believability — the trust a reader has for a medium, format, or phraseology.
- Use "1 + 1 can equal 10" (Reinforcement) when combining emotional images — two joined images can have ten times the impact of either alone.

## Anti-patterns
- **Piling promise upon promise**: builds sales resistance; strengthen the believability-structure of the original promise instead.
- **Attacking a competitor without offering the solution**: evokes skepticism and dislike, not sales.
- **Contradicting accepted images**: people won't believe a product is what it isn't; you cannot break through the walls of a prison.
- **Demanding the prospect jump a believability-chasm**: if you don't build a bridge of ideas starting on his side, the ad fails.
- **Making mechanism copy dull**: mechanism copy is still selling copy; it must be loaded with promise and emotion.
- **Using an empty fifth-stage headline**: a startler, attention-getter, or cute headline with no real identification wastes the client's money.

## Worked Example
The Television Repair Manual ad (1951) demonstrates Gradualization, Redefinition, and Concentration working together. The power-claim headline "Save up to $100 a Year on Your TV Repairs!" failed because prospects saw themselves as non-handymen. The revised ad opened with a universally-accepted resentment headline — "WHY HAVEN'T TV OWNERS BEEN TOLD THESE FACTS?" — then built acceptance step by step: an inclusion question ("Was your set purchased after the spring of 1947?" — 95% answer yes), a first promise, symptom descriptions that coax "Yes... Yes... Yes," proof of the set's dependability, and finally the redefinition of "repairs" as "minor adjustments on the outside controls." Only after the reader accepted that his set was dependable and that he could fix it himself was the money-saving claim brought in at full power.

## Key Takeaways
1. Body copy sells by altering the prospect's vision of reality across three dimensions: desire, identification, belief.
2. Intensify desire with fresh fulfillments; never repeat the same phraseology or you lose the reader.
3. Build a saleable personality (Identification) when price and function no longer differentiate.
4. Make claims believable before you state them (Gradualization) — structure is the architecture of belief.
5. Redefine objections away: simplify the hard, escalate the unimportant, reduce the expensive.
6. Prove the mechanism ("How does it work?") — name it, describe it, or feature it depending on sophistication.
7. Concentrate to destroy alternate channels, and camouflage to borrow the medium's stored believability.
