# Copywriting Master

The agentic engine for producing excellent copy. It turns a brief into finished copy by running a seven-step loop: gather, position, desire, structure, write, humanize, verify.

## How to Use This Skill

- **With a brief** (product, audience, channel, goal): run the full Agentic Copywriting Loop below.
- **With a topic**: read the relevant chapter from the Chapter Index to ground the approach.
- **With a chapter number**: load that chapter file from `chapters/chNN.md`.
- **With an existing page**: audit what already converts before writing. If the page already has strong proof sections (worked examples, testimonials, per-feature story blocks), keep them and write only the missing persuasion beats — the hero hook, the cost-of-inaction (Angst) section, and the closing CTA. Do not rewrite good copy for the sake of it; on a redesign the loop's value is the strategy and the missing beats, not a full rewrite of what already works.

## The Agentic Copywriting Loop

Run these steps in order. Do not skip to writing.

### Step 1 GATHER
Extract from the brief: the offer (what is sold, price, guarantee), the audience (who, their pains, their current beliefs), the channel (email, landing page, ad, social), the goal (click, buy, sign up, share), and any voice or brand constraints. Write these down before anything else.

For website work, define the **page's decision**, not only its topic or button click: page type, visitor's likely arrival intent and existing awareness, provider/offer being chosen, main reason to choose it, doubts to resolve, available evidence and primary next action. Use the brief and acquisition context; clarify consequential uncertainty rather than assuming every visitor needs introductory education. Record a sentence such as: “For a homeowner seeking termite treatment, this page must make this provider's approach, recurrence prevention and reasons to choose them credible enough to request an assessment.” This is a strategy statement, not public copy or a claim about unverified services.

Distinguish service/product pages, comparison pages and educational articles. A commercial service page makes the provider's solution and value clear. An article can primarily answer an informational question and connect readers to the appropriate service. Agree the page's decision with the UX outline; do not turn a service page into an article merely because the topic offers many things to explain.

Connect the site's primary outcome to each page's role and each section's contribution. For lead-generation work, define the kind of enquiry and what follows it; for ecommerce, distinguish a product-page action from a completed purchase. Educational, support and utility pages retain their own useful tasks. Draft this mapping from the existing brief and review it in the existing UX/copy round, rather than asking for a new workshop.

### Step 2 POSITION
Use the market-sophistication spectrum to decide what the copy must do. The more aware the market, the less you can rely on novelty and the more you must differentiate or claim.

- **Unaware**: educate. The market does not know it has the problem.
- **Problem-aware**: name the problem, then present the solution category.
- **Solution-aware**: differentiate your solution from the known alternatives.
- **Product-aware**: claim. The market knows your product; remind and reinforce.
- **Most aware**: remind. The market knows everything; just close.

### Step 3 DESIRE
Anchor the copy in the dominant desire. Pick the one that drives this audience hardest and make it the emotional spine of the piece.

1. Survival, enjoyment of life, life extension
2. Enjoyment of food and beverages
3. Freedom from fear, pain, and danger
4. Sexual companionship
5. Comfortable living conditions
6. Be superior, winning, keeping up with the Joneses
7. Care and protection of loved ones
8. Social approval

### Step 4 STRUCTURE
Pick a persuasion arc and map the piece before writing.

- **Long-form**: the 7-A arc: Attention, Acknowledgement, Aggravate, Authority, Angst, Ambiguity, Appeal.
- **Direct response**: a sales-letter formula (headline, hook, problem, solution, proof, offer, guarantee, CTA).
- Map each section to a line in the outline. Know where the CTA lands before you write.

For each commercial-page section, state the buyer question or objection, the provider-specific answer/evidence and how it advances the page's decision. “About termites” is a topic, not a persuasion job. A process section can explain what this company does and what the customer can expect, using verified service details. If a section only adds general subject knowledge, move it to an article or a compact linked callout unless that knowledge directly resolves a buying objection. Use supported process/benefit content when proof is unavailable; never invent differentiators, reviews, guarantees or treatment outcomes.

Plan how copy and visuals carry the argument together: use relevant photographs, diagrams, product views, illustrations or evidence where they communicate more clearly than another paragraph. Write copy to fit the selected visual composition and reading sequence. Images need a specific role and production/source plan; decorative icons do not automatically resolve an overly text-heavy page. Give text-led sections a deliberate hierarchy rather than defaulting to equal cards and generic paragraphs.

### Step 5 WRITE
Write the hook or headline, the body, and the CTA.

- Headlines: use the swipeable formulas from ch13 (see Core Frameworks).
- Place actions for the visitor's readiness and channel. On commercial web pages, give ready-to-act visitors a clear early CTA, then repeat it at useful decision points and the close. Longer persuasion copy may need more argument before its main ask. Do not force every visitor through the whole page before they can act.

### Step 6 HUMANIZE
Apply the voice rules. Vary sentence length. Be specific with numbers and names. Strip every AI tell:

- **Banned AI-isms**: delve, landscape, tapestry, testament, underscore, pivotal, crucial, vibrant, showcase, foster, enhance, leverage, navigate, nuanced, intricate, multifaceted, realm, journey, game-changer, at the end of the day, when it comes to, in a world where, moving forward, circle back, deep dive, lets dive in, let me be clear, make no mistake, it turns out, unpack, straightforward.
- **Banned sentence openers**: Interestingly, Importantly, Notably, Crucially, Essentially, Ultimately, So, Look.
- **Banned patterns**: rule of three; em-dash overuse; -ing appendix clauses; serves as / stands as; false ranges; synonym cycling; signposting; rhetorical-question-answered-immediately; reassurance kickers; generic upbeat closers; sycophancy; hedging stacks; knowledge-cutoff disclaimers; filler.

### Step 7 VERIFY
Self-audit the finished copy. Check it against the anti-AI tells above and against the brief: does it hit the dominant desire, is the CTA clear, is it specific with real numbers and names? Fix anything that fails. Return the finished copy plus a short note on the positioning, desire, and arc choices you made.

Audit purpose before polishing prose: does the page advance its recorded decision, and does each substantial section contribute? Identify topic exposition that displaced the offer, provider's approach, credible reasons to choose them or the next action. For a commercial page, ask whether a competitor could substitute its name without changing the argument; strengthen supported specificity rather than adding invented claims. Keep the strategy rationale and missing-input notes in the review, outside customer-facing copy.

Check that CTA labels, destinations and next-step expectations agree. Every section should contribute to the intended task, but does not need its own CTA. Preserve the reader's ability to learn or compare where that is the page's role; assess persuasion within that context.

## Core Frameworks

- **Market sophistication**: Use the awareness level of the market to set the job of the copy. Unaware markets need education; aware markets need a claim. The claim is the single most important sentence: the specific, believable promise of the result the product delivers. Write the claim before the copy.
- **The dominant desire**: Use it to drive every headline and benefit. People buy to satisfy a deep want, not to own a product. Sell the outcome, not the feature.
- **Means-End Chain**: Use it to trace a feature to the emotional end it serves. Feature leads to benefit leads to the desire. Write the chain out for the offer before drafting.
- **The 17 principles**: Use them as a checklist for the body: specific claims beat vague ones, concrete numbers beat adjectives, a strong guarantee removes risk, urgency creates action, and proof (testimonials, results) outsells assertion.
- **The 7-A arc**: Use for long-form. Attention (stop them), Acknowledgement (name their situation), Aggravate (make the pain vivid), Authority (prove credibility), Angst (show the cost of inaction), Ambiguity (open the possibility of a better outcome), Appeal (the close).
- **Sales-letter formula**: Use for direct response. Headline, hook, problem, solution, proof, offer, guarantee, CTA. Adapt action placement to channel and visitor readiness; commercial web pages retain a clear early route for ready-to-act visitors.
- **Headline formulas (ch13)**: Use the swipeable patterns: the how-to, the list, the question, the before-and-after, the specific-number, the curiosity gap, the fear, the desire, the testimonial, the command. Keep the {placeholder} structure and fill with specifics.

## Scope and Limits

This skill produces copy for marketing and direct response. It is not a substitute for legal review of claims, and it does not verify product facts or compliance with advertising regulations. The frameworks assume a real offer with real proof; do not invent testimonials, numbers, or results. For regulated categories (health, finance, legal), have the copy reviewed before publishing. The chapter files are reference material; the loop above is the operating procedure.
